I was talking to a colleague today when I heard the career center at my college 
recommends applying to **30-50 jobs per day** as a senior. To me, this was a really 
shocking number. I probably could not apply to 30 jobs in a day even if I had the whole 
day to myself, locked in a room with no responsibilities.

I think a lot of career advice I hear these days feels just like this. "No one reads 
anything," "just submit a bunch of applications with AI," "tailor your résumé with 
keywords so the machine picks it up." In this era of automation and economic upheaval, 
people are trying desperately to figure out how to get a job in the old system when, in 
fact, the old system is in the process of falling apart.

Then there's the grifters: Handshake in my emails sending me "fresh new jobs" that are 
no longer accepting responses with a posting that is woefully out of date, Indeed buying 
Glassdoor and ruining any chance they have of independently and honestly discussing 
salary/working conditions, and finally the endless LinkedIn premium pop-ups. Maybe the 
best way to make money is to not get a job, but to tell people that you have jobs for 
them... If only they buy our new subscription offering!

I don't want to say that all of this advice, or all of these tools are completely 
useless. I certainly tailor my résumé based on the application, but I don't do it to 
fight keyword bots. I think what this kind of career advice misses is that one of the 
most important things you need as a young person is finding a job you actually want to work. 
You need to have a clear destination, and you need to take steps towards that dream 
career.

I want to provide a version of career advice that has been most effective in my 
anecdotal experience for finding a job that is specific and related to what you are 
looking for.  In my experience as a college student navigating the job market, the best 
method for finding work you love is about setting yourself up to find people, giving 
them an opportunity to reach out to you, and having a place to give prospective 
recruiters a way to understand who you are and what you stand for.

# 1. The best jobs are the kind that come looking for you.

In my college career, the best job offers I've gotten have come from my "weak ties." 
It's certainly true that I got fewer opportunities this way, but the opportunities I did 
get were a much better fit.

For example, my [summer research](/blog/human-app-ai-world/) group. I got this offer after talking to one of my 
professors about wanting to work in the nonprofit software space. He ended up talking 
about another professor at the college (who I never had but had read a research poster 
about) and recommended we talk. I ended up joining that student cohort during the year 
and working little by little on the software in my free time. I soon learned that there 
were slots open for a paid summer research internship program. I applied, and was 
accepted with his recommendation.

The moral of this story is that you should tell people the kind of jobs that you want. 
Especially people in your field/department. This is one of the best
ways to find opportunities that really mesh well with your values.
Plus, it will help that you already have a recommendation before
you even think about writing the application.

# 2. Talk to everyone!

"Networking" is such a terrible word. It is basically a zhuzhed-up word that means 
"talking." You are already networking all the time without realizing it. All the social 
events you go to, all of the birthday parties, movies, plays... All of these are great 
opportunities to network. Especially if you are a 20-something-college-student, you are 
probably going around and meeting new people all the time, and having little 
conversations.

This probably sounds like bad dating advice—because it is bad advice if you're 
trying to *date*—but if you feel even a tiny bit of connection, recognition, or 
understanding with a stranger you really need to give them your email address. I 
find that business cards[^1] are a great way to do this, especially if you have a blank area 
to write what you connected about and give them a good prompt to email you about later. 

Much like in dating, it's important that you *give* them information rather than asking 
for their information. Even though it does mean you might get fewer successful contacts, 
it means that they can make the decision of whether to reach out to you. If they throw 
away your card, no harm, no foul!

This is also a slick way to have people reach out to you with more pertinent 
information. Instead of sending emails like "hi :)" they will send you emails like 
"Disability Advocacy Group - Need Website - Will Pay." As much as I love an adorable "hi 
:)" email, I am making the Mr. Krabs dollar sign eyes when I get the second email. You 
will be surprised how well this strategy works. I definitely was (that second email was 
real, by the way!)

# 3. Show me the real *you*.

There's one more thing that you miss when doing a traditional application. I think a lot 
of people really under-appreciate the value of the little "portfolio website link" box. 

Think: Most of the application is designed and formatted by the employer. Your beauty 
and uniqueness has a 200-character limit and the text box you're writing it in WILL 
delete your hard-fought answer if you so much as click at it the wrong way. So, there's 
a huge amount of power in creating your own website. Obviously, or you wouldn't be 
reading this right now!

If you are reading this article, you should create your own website right this instant. 
Get an HTML coding book from your local library. Or, you can stick it to the
librarians and use ChatGPT—though I definitely think the book version
is more informative, there are many easy to follow guides that are
full of pictures and examples.

Even if you *think* you don't know how to code, you have to try. I am saying this because it 
really has the power to change your life for the better. Start small, just with a layout 
of your name, a photo if you are comfortable, a description with your goals and 
interests, and finally a couple of important links to the places you already create.

The difference between having your own capital-H HTML website and having a social media 
or a linktree or a canva page is that you are completely in charge. It is the way that 
YOU decide how you should look on the internet. It also says a lot about who you are as 
a person, not just in the explicit content, but in the choices you make with the design, 
the fonts, the colors, etc.

It also is something that you own *financially*. No one else will put tracking code on 
it, no one else will advertise their service on it, it is entirely run by you. This 
might sound expensive, but actually the website you're reading this on costs me exactly 
$10 each year. That's not even $1/month, which will probably shock you if you've ever 
gotten a squarespace ad on a podcast.[^2]

Once you have a basic website, you can expand it with a portfolio, about pages, blog 
posts, and anything else you can think of. Then, you can show off who you really are, 
and all of the work you do that isn't necessarily résumé 
worthy.

It doesn't need to be perfect. You don't even need to think it's good. Honestly, there are many parts of this website that I feel need work, but when I show
it to people they tend to be impressed and don't notice the flaws.
You will always be your own worst critic, 
and having your own website at all is really a power move in the business world, even if 
it is just a short description and a link to your résumé and portfolio.

You will be not just the boss of your own place on the .net, you will be *The Boss* 
when people look at your website and see how cool it is. Try it!

# You got this!

Hopefully some of this advice was helpful to you[^3]. I know it is really difficult to 
figure this stuff out when you're just entering the job market at this time.

Here are some action items for you to really put this into practice:
- Reach out to someone that can connect you to your dream job: email a professor, 
message an alum on LinkedIn
- Design your business cards, even if you don't print them. You can find templates 
online, my college also offers affordable print services for college-branded cards. 
Yours might too, and if not you can always visit Staples or print them online.
- Create an about page with HTML, even if you don't publish it. Check out 
"CoderDojo: Build Your Own Website"... it's literally the book I learned to code with 
when I was younger. Owning your site is power!

Most importantly, remember that everything will work out eventually. Think carefully 
about what your dream job/best career looks like, and make sure that you are working 
towards it, and not just treading water.

Please [let me know](/about) how it goes! You got this!
