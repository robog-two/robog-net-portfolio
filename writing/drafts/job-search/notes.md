# Interview notes: job search post (working title TBD)

Captured from Sam's own words (voice transcription, so quotes are rough). Not
interpretation. Anything model-added is marked **[model]**.

## Trigger
- About an hour before the interview (2026-09-29), heard that the college career center
  was telling senior capstone students to apply to **30–50 jobs per day**.
- Reaction: "insane," "terrible job advice," "a waste of people's time."
- "I couldn't apply to 30 to 50... if I had the whole day free." Max "one to two per hour."
- Time goes to: figuring out what to apply to / whether it's a good fit, background
  research on the company, tailoring the resume, cover letter stuff.
- Mass-applying gets you "slop jobs": you "email blast" your resume, the hiring manager
  thinks "I guess this person looks fine." Not tailored to your interests, not "a special
  place to work," not somewhere that makes you feel "accomplished and fulfilled."
- Students hearing this feel the market is brutal: "finding a job is so hard," "apply to
  nine million things," "you're always getting rejected."

## Claim (Sam's words)
- "The secret is just leaving that traditional marketplace."
- "The best jobs you don't apply to." The best jobs "you're a good fit for and the person
  who's running it finds out about you."
- "The true power is having people come to you instead of you going to them."
- "It's personal branding really." Asking people, getting involved, and website/cards
  are all personal branding.
- "People really undervalue being noticeable."
- Applies to more people than career offices "would like you to think." There's "an
  economy and a market and a norm" around applications, application help, and resume
  tailoring. "All of these are good things, but my resume is not what got me the job, my
  resume is just another link on my website." (Still important to keep it up to date.)
- "The number one thing that works for me is building these connections and also having
  this kind of body of work that I can point to."

## Leverage / self-presentation
- "I have so much more leverage when people come to see my website versus when I try to
  boil myself down into an information box on an application."
- "I get to present the information about myself" the way you see yourself: "You're
  getting a picture of how I see myself," "actually me and not the boiled down fake
  version of me for your application."
- "Level the playing field."
- Especially for students without traditional experience (e.g. working at a coffee
  shop). Experience is "overvalued... but that's just the way it is." "It may not look
  like I've done much of this work on paper, but here's six links you can look at." They
  "are full of work even if it wasn't at this one specific company."
- Ladder: links lead to internship-style opportunities, which lead to "more official,"
  "polished" resume items.

## Weak ties
- "I would totally describe them as weak ties." "None of these were people that I really
  knew closely... very far out connections."
- Came from "expressing to people that I knew in my life... here's what I'm looking for.
  Do you know anything about that?" and from "being involved in things."
- **[model, research]** Mark Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties," *American Journal of
  Sociology* 78(6), 1973. In his sample of people who found jobs through contacts, most
  saw that contact only "occasionally" or "rarely," not "often." Follow-up book: *Getting
  a Job* (1974). Verify exact figures against the paper before citing.

## Networking
- People think networking means "wear a suit" at "a corporate event." It's really "just
  the concept of talking to people," with a "zhuzh up business word to make it sound
  fancy."
- **What makes it networking and not just talking:** "You have to give them a way to come
  back to you... You have to give them a lead."
  - "Here's my business card."
  - A page on the website about a specific thing you're doing.
  - A blog post about a presentation you gave on your research.
  - A portfolio showing artwork and projects.
  - GitHub: the software you've written, "how I approach writing code," open-source
    interests.
- "Most people already have a lot of these things. You just have to put them together in
  a cohesive way, in a cohesive space."
- Who to reach out to: email a professor ("do you know anyone who is interested in XYZ
  type of work"), a friend of a friend, "your uncle who works in a different field but
  goes to a lot of conferences." "Leveraging your existing people."

## Start small and let it compound
- "I didn't do this overnight." A big hub all at once is "a really daunting task and you
  really shouldn't be doing that."
- Start with a website with a simple about page: "what I'm working on, here's what I'm
  interested in, here's some links."
- Add a blog when "I really want to share my ideas about this."
- Add a portfolio when you love your drawings from drawing class and want to share them
  more easily. Later a job asks "where can we see your art?" and you can print the
  portfolio page.
- "It compounds on itself." "Little by little," "very opportunistic." "Everybody's always
  doing things... say hey I've done this thing, now I want to share it."
- Tools: "11ty templates" (Eleventy) or whatever; the tool doesn't matter. There was
  another word here the transcription garbled ("podcode"?).
- People say "wow, your website is really nice." Sam doesn't think it's that nice: it
  "needs so much work," and Sam wishes there were more time for polish.
- Sam's dad was mentioned here (details unclear).
- Wants people to "feel very empowered to create their own web content, web presence."
- **Push business cards.** "They've done a lot for me." Sam hands them out to people met
  at any event.
- Even a LinkedIn or Instagram page with an email and a portfolio counts.

## Where readers should end up
- "I like this way more than logging into Handshake," a "garbage website that's just
  trying to sell you stuff" and "doesn't actually care about finding you a job."
- Actually email a professor, friend of a friend, or uncle.
- Give people something to come back to.
